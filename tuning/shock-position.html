Shock position

Where each shock mounts on the tower (top) and on the arm (bottom) changes the effective spring rate and how much that end rolls.

Last edited: November 26, 2026 · 1/10 off-road buggies, dirt and clay, 17.5 stock class

Starting points, not gospel. These effects are general tuning principles and the numbers are starting ranges, not measured results from this garage. Change one thing at a time, and change the front and rear separately.

Shock tops (tower)

Where the top of each shock mounts on its tower changes the effective spring rate and how much that end rolls.

Each box shows what happens at that end of the car. Front and rear often do opposite things.

Front end

Tops further apart (more upright)

  • Steering: Quicker response; sharp on smooth, high-bite clay.
  • Traction: Less front grip over bumps.
  • Stability: Less roll and dive; flatter in corners.

Tops closer together (more lean-in)

  • Steering: More front grip and steering on bumpy or low-bite tracks.
  • Traction: More compliance over bumps.
  • Stability: More roll and dive; slower to react.

Rear end

Tops further apart (more upright)

  • Steering: More rotation on power; the rear gets lively.
  • Traction: Less rear grip, especially over bumps and on loose dirt.
  • Stability: Flatter on jumps; resists squat.

Tops closer together (more lean-in)

  • Steering: A calmer rear; the car can push on exit.
  • Traction: More rear traction on exit and over bumps.
  • Stability: More squat and roll.

More upright is firmer and more linear; more lean-in is softer and more progressive. Use the top position together with the bottom position: moving both the same way is a bigger change than moving one.

Shock bottoms (arm)

Where the bottom of each shock mounts on its arm also changes the effective spring rate.

Each box shows what happens at that end of the car. Front and rear often do opposite things.

Front end

Bottom further out (toward the wheel)

  • Steering: Quicker response and more direct steering.
  • Traction: Less front grip over bumps.
  • Stability: Less roll; a firmer front end.

Bottom further in (toward the chassis)

  • Steering: More front grip and a softer feel through the corner.
  • Traction: More compliance over bumps.
  • Stability: More roll and dive.

Rear end

Bottom further out (toward the wheel)

  • Steering: More rotation on power.
  • Traction: Less rear grip over bumps.
  • Stability: A firmer rear; flatter on jumps.

Bottom further in (toward the chassis)

  • Steering: A calmer rear; may push on exit.
  • Traction: More rear grip over bumps and on exit.
  • Stability: More squat and roll.

Bottom and top positions add together. Outward at the bottom plus upright at the top is the firmest setting; inward and lean-in is the softest.

Using top and bottom together

Treat the two as one adjustment. Moving the top inward and the bottom inward both soften that end; moving both outward both firm it up. Moving one in and the other out cancels part of the change.

What you are looking for

You want each end to move just enough to find grip without feeling lazy. On smooth, high-bite clay, lean toward firm and upright. If the car pushes or skips over bumps, soften the position at the end that is struggling before you change springs.
