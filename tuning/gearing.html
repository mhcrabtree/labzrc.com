Gearing

Gearing sets the balance between acceleration and top speed. It is set at the motor, so it affects the front and rear ends together.

Last edited: November 26, 2026 · 1/10 off-road buggies, dirt and clay, 17.5 stock class

Starting points, not gospel. These effects are general tuning principles and the numbers are starting ranges, not measured results from this garage. Change one thing at a time, and change the front and rear separately.

What the numbers mean

Final drive ratio (FDR) is spur teeth ÷ pinion teeth × the car's internal ratio. The internal ratio is built into the car after the spur gear, so it differs from model to model; your manual gives it, usually next to a gear chart.

Higher final drive ratio (bigger spur or smaller pinion)

  • Acceleration: More punch out of corners.
  • Top speed: Lower.
  • Temperature: Cooler motor and ESC.

Lower final drive ratio (smaller spur or bigger pinion)

  • Acceleration: Less punch.
  • Top speed: Higher on long straights.
  • Temperature: Hotter motor and ESC.

Picking a starting ratio

  • A 17.5 is a milder motor than many kits assume, which points toward a higher final drive ratio than a hot motor would use on the same track.
  • On a small, tight track, start near the middle of your manual's chart and move one tooth at a time, judging by motor temperature and the end of the main straight.
  • After a run, check motor and ESC temperature. If either is too hot to hold your hand on for a few seconds, gear lower (higher ratio). If the car runs cool and feels slow at the end of the straight, gear up (lower ratio).
  • Set the gear mesh with a sheet of paper between the gears.

Front vs rear

  • 2WD: gearing affects only the rear wheels, which are the driven ones.
  • 4WD: one pinion drives both ends through the center shaft. Some 4WD cars let you run the front driveline slightly faster or slower than the rear; that is a car-specific setting, so check your manual.
