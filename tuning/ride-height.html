Ride height and droop

Ride height is how high the chassis sits at each end. Droop is how far each arm can drop below that.

Each box shows what happens at that end of the car. Front and rear often do opposite things.

Front end

Higher front ride height

  • Steering: Less steering response; more roll.
  • Traction: Cleaner over bumps; less front grip on smooth ground.
  • Stability: More roll and dive; less stable.

Lower front ride height

  • Steering: Sharper steering from a lower center of gravity.
  • Traction: More front grip on smooth tracks.
  • Stability: Flatter and more stable; can drag or bottom on bumps.

Rear end

Higher rear ride height

  • Steering: More rotation; the car gets looser.
  • Traction: Cleaner over bumps; less rear grip on smooth ground.
  • Stability: More squat and roll.

Lower rear ride height

  • Steering: Calmer and more stable.
  • Traction: More rear grip on smooth tracks.
  • Stability: Flatter; can drag on jump faces.

Set ride height first because it changes every other setting. Droop works with it: more droop lets the wheel follow bumps and ruts for grip; less droop keeps the car flatter and more predictable. Check the front and rear separately.

How to set it

  1. Measure ride height with the car ready to run (battery in, body on), on a flat surface, with a ride height gauge.
  2. Set the front and rear to the manufacturer's number first, then adjust one end at a time.
  3. Check that the car sits level side to side, then check droop at each end by lifting the chassis and measuring how far the arms drop.
